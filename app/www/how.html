How the model works — all tabs (equations, assumptions, sources, symbols are defined in the Glossary tab)

Interactive model. All parameter values and units are illustrative placeholders chosen to make the qualitative patterns visible, they are not fitted to any data set. The structure is a minimal synthesis loosely based on the papers credited below.

① Static physiology

Carbon. Fixed C: \(A = A_{\max} L\). Mycorrhizal plants pass a fraction \(c\kappa\) to the fungus: \(C_{NM}=A,\; C_{AM}=A(1-c\kappa)\).

Phosphorus. Saturating uptake by roots and by hyphae (hyphae scavenge better at low P, \(K_f < K_r\)): $$U_r=u_r\frac{S}{K_r+S},\qquad U_f=u_f\frac{S}{K_f+S}$$ $$P_{NM}=U_r,\qquad P_{AM}=(1-\rho c)\,U_r + c\,\phi\,\max(U_f-U_0,\,0)$$ where \(\rho\) is down-regulation of the direct root pathway and \(\phi\) the share of fungal P passed to the host. The fungus first keeps its own P need \(U_0\) (per unit colonisation) and passes on only the surplus; \(U_0=0\) gives the model without a hump. \(S\) = soil-available P; \(P_{AM},P_{NM}\) = phosphorus acquired (in this app P always means phosphorus, never a fungal density). \(K_r,K_f\) are half-saturation constants, not carrying capacities.

Growth = smoothed Liebig co-limitation, biomass \(B\) (strict minimum as \(n\to\infty\)): $$B=\operatorname{softmin}\!\left(Y C,\;\frac{P}{\theta}\right),\qquad \operatorname{softmin}(x,y)=\left(x^{-n}+y^{-n}\right)^{-1/n}$$ The plant is called C-limited if \(YC < P/\theta\), otherwise P-limited.

Mycorrhizal growth response and classification $$\mathrm{MGR}=\ln\frac{B_{AM}}{B_{NM}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \begin{cases}\text{mutualism} & \mathrm{MGR}>\epsilon\\ \text{commensalism} & |\mathrm{MGR}|\le\epsilon\\ \text{parasitism} & \mathrm{MGR}<-\epsilon\end{cases}$$ Intuition: when P limits growth, extra fungal P raises growth (MGR > 0); when C limits growth, the C paid to the fungus reduces it (strict-Liebig limit: \(\mathrm{MGR}=\ln(1-c\kappa)<0\)). Hump along fertility. With \(U_0>0\), MGR peaks at intermediate \(S\): in very P-poor soil the hyphae take up less than the fungus's own need (\(U_f\le U_0\)), nothing is passed on and MGR \(=\ln(1-\rho c)<0\); at intermediate \(S\) the surplus P pays; at high \(S\) the plant is C-limited and the C paid makes MGR negative again. At the defaults (\(U_0=0.3\)): parasitism for \(S<1.4\), mutualism for \(1.5<S<19.5\) with a peak MGR = 0.47 at \(S\approx4.2\), parasitism for \(S>28.6\) (commensal in between). This matches the field pattern summarised by Grasso et al. (2025, citing Albornoz et al. 2021): no P benefit in extremely P-poor soils, and suppressed associations in P-rich soils. With \(U_0=0\), MGR falls monotonically with \(S\). Edge cases: \(c=0\) is shown as “no colonisation / neutral”; with \(c=\rho=1,\ \phi=0\) the AM plant acquires no P and MGR \(\to-\infty\) (clipped in the plots).

Benefit vs. cost (plot ②): benefit \(=B(P_{AM},C_{NM})-B_{NM}\) (P effect alone); cost \(=B(P_{AM},C_{NM})-B_{AM}\) (C effect); net \(=B_{AM}-B_{NM}\). The split is evaluated P first; the reverse order gives the same net, but a different split.

Effective exchange rate (market view): extra P gained per unit C paid, $$T_{\text{eff}}=\frac{c\phi \max(U_f-U_0,0)-\rho c U_r}{c\kappa A}\quad[\text{P per C}].$$

Fungal side (plot ⑤, speculative). Fungal growth \(F = Y_f\,\operatorname{softmin}\!\big(c\kappa A+\beta_f,\;\big[c\,U_f-c\,\phi\max(U_f-U_0,0)\big]/\theta_f\big)\), response \(\Delta_F=\ln(F/F_{\text{ref}})\) where \(F_{\text{ref}}\) is the fitness the fungus would get with a reference partner (AMF are obligate, so “no partner” = zero is no useful baseline). Sign pairs (with the same ±ε band) give: (+,+) mutualism, (0,+) commensalism, (−,+) fungus parasitic, (+,−) plant exploits fungus, (−,−) competition, (0,−) amensalism. The fungal C income \(c\kappa A\) does not depend on P delivered, so \(\Delta_F\) is often flat in \(S\) and its sign, hence the quadrant label, is set by the arbitrary \(F_{\text{ref}}\).

② Population dynamics

Neuhauser & Fargione (2004), eq. (1), in this model’s notation (paper: \(P\to M\), \(K\to K_P\), \(a,b,d,e\to a_M,b_M,d_M,e_M\)): host \(H\), mutualist \(M\) $$\frac{dH}{dt}=rH\left(1-\frac{H}{K_P+\gamma M}\right)-a_MHM,\qquad \frac{dM}{dt}=b_MHM-d_MM(1+e_MM).$$ \(\gamma M\) is the host’s gain (raised carrying capacity), \(a_M\) the cost to the host. Assumes \(K_P>d_M/b_M\); otherwise the fungus cannot persist and the app shows no classification. Equilibrium criterion: compare \(H^*\) with the host-only equilibrium \(K_P\) (shown as \(\ln(H^*/K_P)\) with the ±ε band – the band is new here). Pulse criterion: sign of \(J_{HM}=\partial\dot H/\partial M\) at equilibrium, $$J_{HM}=\left.\frac{\partial \dot H}{\partial M}\right|_* = H^*\left[\frac{r\gamma H^*}{(K_P+\gamma M^*)^2}-a_M\right],$$ equivalent to the equilibrium lying below (mutualism) or above (parasitism) the vertex of the host isocline, \(\big(K_P/2+\gamma r/4a_M+a_MK_P^2/4r\gamma,\; r/2a_M-K_P/2\gamma\big)\). Since the equilibrium lies on the host isocline, both criteria reduce to exact rules for \(M^*\): $$\text{pulse-mutualistic}\iff M^*<\frac{r}{2a_M}-\frac{K_P}{2\gamma},\qquad \text{equilibrium-mutualistic}\ (H^*>K_P)\iff M^*<\frac{r}{a_M}-\frac{K_P}{\gamma}.$$ The second bound is twice the first, so along a fertility gradient the pulse criterion always switches first (at defaults: pulse at KP ≈ 3.62, equilibrium at KP ≈ 13.53; marked in the strip plot). \(K_P>\gamma r/a_M\) (vertex below \(M=0\)) is only a sufficient bound: beyond it both criteria are always parasitic. The two criteria can disagree (paper Fig. 1, isocline 2).

Defaults: γ = 5, r = 2, aM = 0.3, bM = 3 from the paper’s examples; dM = 1 and eM = 10 are placeholders (the paper’s tables use d = e = 1, available as a preset; with eM = 1 the pulse criterion is parasitic for every KP). Integration: adaptive RK4 (step doubling, rtol 1e-6), interior equilibrium solved analytically (quadratic). Link to soil P (conceptual): KP stands in for soil fertility; in tab ① this corresponds to raising soil-available P, S. No quantitative mapping KP(S) is implied.

③ Cheater fungus

Neuhauser & Fargione (2004), eq. (2)/(A.1), in this app’s notation (paper: \(P\to M\), \(Q\to Z\), \(K\to K_P\), subscripts \(1\to M\), \(2\to Z\), \(c_1\to\alpha_{MZ}\), \(c_2\to\alpha_{ZM}\)): mutualist \(M\) and cheater \(Z\) (no benefit to the host) compete for the host: $$\frac{dH}{dt}=rH\left(1-\frac{H}{K_P+\gamma M}\right)-a_MMH-a_ZZH$$ $$\frac{dM}{dt}=b_MHM-d_MM(1+e_MM+\alpha_{MZ}Z),\qquad \frac{dZ}{dt}=b_ZHZ-d_ZZ(1+e_ZZ+\alpha_{ZM}M)$$ Competition convention: \(\alpha_{ij}\) = effect of \(j\) on \(i\) (so \(\alpha_{MZ}\), the paper’s \(c_1\), is the effect of the cheater on the mutualist); as in the paper it acts inside the density-dependent death term. The paper assumes \(b_M/d_M>b_Z/d_Z\) (mutualist grows faster). Invasion criteria (App. A.2): \(Z\) invades the mutualist-only equilibrium iff \(\alpha_{ZM}\lt e_Z\lambda_Z^{(M)}/\lambda_M^{(M)}\); \(M\) invades the cheater-only equilibrium iff \(\alpha_{MZ}\lt e_M\lambda_M^{(Z)}/\lambda_Z^{(Z)}\), where \(\lambda_i^{(X)}=\frac{1}{e_i}\big(\frac{b_i}{d_i}\bar H^{(X)}-1\big)\) and \(\bar H^{(X)}\) is the host density at the X-only equilibrium. Increasing \(K_P\) favours the cheater (paper Tables 1–2; the app reproduces Table 2: KP = 20 → H = 2.667, Z = 4.333, M = 0).

Why the plant can do better when the cheater wins. With the paper’s \(e_M=e_Z=1\) the fungi regulate the host from above: \(H^*\) lies on a fungal isocline, \(H^*=\frac{d_i}{b_i}(1+e_iX_i+\dots)\), so it barely depends on \(K_P\) (≈ 2.4–2.8 for KP = 2–40). The mutualist alone is then itself parasitic for KP ≳ 2.4, and because it is the more efficient grower (\(b_M/d_M>b_Z/d_Z\)) it holds the host lower than the cheater does: at KP = 20, H = 2.667 with Z alone vs 2.450 with M alone. “Mutualist” and “cheater” refer to \(\gamma\), not to the realised effect; the readouts and the KP plot show the M-only reference for comparison. The preset “plant-limited” (\(e_M=e_Z=10\), as in tab ②, with \(\alpha_{MZ},\alpha_{ZM}\) scaled by the same factor) gives the intuitive picture: the cheater wins in fertile soil and the plant is worse off than with M alone. Bistability: the KP sweep uses one set of initial densities; the optional invasion sweep (dashed, top strip) starts at the M-only equilibrium with rare Z. Where the two differ, the outcome depends on starting densities (e.g. paper Table 2 at KP = 2: Z cannot invade M alone, yet a coexistence state is also stable).

New here: optional cheater benefit \(\gamma_Z\) (host capacity \(K_P+\gamma M+\gamma_ZZ\); \(\gamma_Z=0\) is the paper); the time-resolved plant response \(\ln(H/H_{NM})\) with \(H_{NM}\) logistic; and a bridge to tab ①: with colonisation split by abundance, \(\phi_{mix}=\frac{M}{M+Z}\phi+\frac{Z}{M+Z}\phi_Z\) is fed into the static MGR. Note: Tables 1–2 use αZM = 0.6128, just above the KP = 2 invasion threshold 0.6127 (rising to ≈ 0.617 as KP → ∞), so convergence there is very slow.

④ Plant sanctions

Inspired by reciprocal rewards (Kiers et al. 2011), partner choice in biological markets and proportional rewards (Wyatt et al. 2014), as summarised by Hammerstein & Noë (2016); not a model from any of the five papers. The plant scales the carbon each fungus receives per unit abundance by a reward weight $$w_i=(1-s)+s\,\frac{q_i}{\bar q},\qquad q_M=\gamma+q_0,\quad q_Z=\gamma_Z+q_0,\qquad \bar q=\frac{Mq_M+Zq_Z}{M+Z},$$ so that \(Mw_M+Zw_Z=M+Z\): sanctions redistribute the reward per unit fungus without changing its total. The host’s actual carbon drain \((a_Mw_MM+a_Zw_ZZ)H\) is not conserved, because \(a_M\ne a_Z\): shifting reward to the cheaper mutualist lowers it (at the demo values to about 0.95–0.999 of the equal-reward drain; shown in the readout), so part of the plant’s gain from sanctions is a cheaper partner mix. \(s=0\): no discrimination; \(s=1\): reward proportional to benefit delivered (plus baseline \(q_0\), which also reflects how well the plant can tell partners apart). Carbon taken and fungal growth both scale with \(w_i\): $$\dot H=rH\Big(1-\tfrac{H}{K_P+\gamma M+\gamma_ZZ}\Big)-a_Mw_MMH-a_Zw_ZZH,$$ $$\dot M=b_Mw_MHM-d_MM(1+e_MM+\alpha_{MZ}Z),\qquad \dot Z=b_Zw_ZHZ-d_ZZ(1+e_ZZ+\alpha_{ZM}M).$$ With a single fungus \(w=1\), so sanctions only act when partners compete. Demo values αMZ = 2.0, αZM = 0.3 are placeholders chosen so that the cheater robustly wins at high KP without sanctions (with the paper’s knife-edge αZM = 0.6128 almost any s > 0 flips the outcome).

⑤ Parameter space

A precomputed Latin-hypercube sample over each tab’s slider ranges (105 sets for ① and ②; 4·104 for ③ and ④; seed 20261104). Rates, costs, yields and half-saturations are log-uniform; fractions, soil P / fertility, γ, α and s are uniform. Each set is classified with the same rules as the corresponding tab (MGR ±ε in ①; ln(H*/KP) ±ε or the pulse sign of JHM in ②; plant response, or who wins, in ③ and ④). The shares are the fraction of those sets in each class.

No live sampling. The app ships integer counts, binned on a grid: 10 bins per axis, 10 fertility bins, 5 light bins (① only), stored for each pair among at most eight parameters per view (fertility, light in ①, and the parameters with highest importance). Conditioning on a fertility (or light) band sums the bins whose intervals overlap the selected range. Cells with fewer than 15 samples are greyed out.

Phase map. The default view colours each cell by its majority class (paler = lower majority share). Choose a single class to colour every cell by that class's share (0–100%) on a sequential scale in the class colour (green = mutualism / mutualist, grey = commensalism, red = parasitism / cheater, dark grey = cannot persist / no fungus, amber = coexistence). Hover shows the share and the sample count n. Cells with fewer than 15 samples stay grey.

Caveat. The percentages describe the slider box in placeholder units; they are not frequencies in nature and they change if the ranges or the sampling scale change. Variable importance is random-forest permutation importance on the same sample (top parameter = 1).

Credits (conceptual sources)

  • Neuhauser & Fargione (2004), Ecol. Model. 177: 337–352 — host–symbiont model with both benefit and cost; rising soil fertility turns mutualism into parasitism.
  • Hochberg, Gomulkiewicz, Holt & Thompson (2000), J. Evol. Biol. 13: 213–222 — habitat productivity gradients: mutualists favoured in poor/sink habitats, virulent symbionts in productive ones.
  • Schwartz & Hoeksema (1998), Ecology 79: 1029–1038 — biological-market / comparative-advantage view; trade is mutualistic only inside a window of exchange rates; P fertilisation shrinks gains.
  • Grasso, Ryan, Albornoz & Renton (2025), New Phytol. 248: 1429–1441 — Liebig co-limitation and one-to-one C-for-P trade; giving C is costly only when C limits growth.
  • Hammerstein & Noë (2016), Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 371: 20150101 — biological trade and markets: partner choice, sanctions, exchange rates.
